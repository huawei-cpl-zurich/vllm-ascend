# PREFLOW: Safe preemption for LLM prefill

Preemption fixes head-of-line blocking. It also decides who pays for the
queue. Every time a scheduler moves a short prompt forward, some other prompt
waits longer. Under shortest-remaining-processing-time (SRPT), that trade can be
excellent for the mean and brutal for an individual long request.

We see both sides in our synthetic long-context stress test. SRPT improves mean
time to first token (TTFT) by 84.6% over FCFS, but the worst matched request
takes 7.52× as long as it does under FCFS. PREFLOW with 120% slack retains a
78.2% mean improvement and limits the observed worst matched slowdown to 2.37×.
With 30% slack, it still improves mean TTFT by 68.2%, while the observed worst
slowdown is 1.37×.

Can we keep most of the benefit of preemption without arbitrarily sacrificing
long requests?

PREFLOW separates that question into two decisions. A stretch-oriented,
work-aware efficiency objective decides which request should run next. A
separate feasibility test decides whether allowing that action is still safe
for every request already in the system. High-value work can overtake, but only
while the queue has enough slack to absorb the delay.

## The synthetic stress test

The workload is deliberately severe. It mixes prompt lengths that differ by
25× and sends them through bursty arrival phases so that queue ordering has a
visible effect. It is not a persistent-overload experiment: queues build during
bursts and later drain.

- The trace contains 1,000 requests generated by a Markov-modulated Poisson
  process, averaging 0.5 requests/s over a 2,000-second arrival span.
- Prompt buckets are 4K, 8K, 16K, 32K, 65K, and 100K tokens, with shares of
  30%, 25%, 20%, 14%, 8%, and 3%.
- Each request asks for 128 output tokens.
- One TP=2 prefill instance and one TP=2 decode instance run across four Ascend
  NPUs. Prefill uses fixed 4,096-token chunks, eight resident requests, and one
  scheduled request per step.
- The model is Qwen3-30B-A3B-Instruct-2507 with dummy weight loading. Prefix
  caching is disabled.

The six prompt classes span 25× in token count, but FCFS compresses their mean
TTFT into a narrow 116.6–123.8 second range. This does not mean their execution
costs are equal. During bursts, queueing dominates total TTFT strongly enough
to hide much of the underlying cost difference.

PREFLOW-50 lets short requests pass while earlier deadlines still have enough
slack. Mean TTFT falls to 5.0 seconds for 4K prompts and then grows with prompt
size, reaching 186.9 seconds at 100K. PREFLOW does not remove congestion; it
redistributes waiting time. Short prompts benefit dramatically, while the
largest prompts absorb more delay. This is the benefit/harm tradeoff examined
later in the post. The grouped bars use observed wall-clock TTFT from the same
matched benchmark requests.

![FCFS and PREFLOW-50 mean wall-clock TTFT by prompt length](benchmark_output/final-synthetic-policy-suite/analysis/06_mean_ttft_by_prompt_length.png)

All 11 policy configurations completed the same 1,000 requests with identical
request IDs, prompt lengths, and scheduled arrival times. This is a controlled
single-seed experiment, not a claim about every production workload. Its job is
to expose the scheduling tradeoff cleanly.

## Why aging is not a contract

Aging is a common way to soften starvation in work-aware scheduling.
[PrefillOnly](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.07203) is the representative aging
baseline in this experiment: predicted work favors short requests, while a
linear waiting-time term gradually raises the priority of requests left behind.

The coefficient $\lambda$ controls how quickly waiting overcomes the work
ranking. A small value stays close to shortest-work scheduling. A large value
pushes the policy back toward FCFS. This creates an empirical
efficiency/protection tradeoff, but $\lambda$ does not specify a maximum
per-request slowdown. The effective protection also changes with the arrival
process, prompt distribution, chunk size, and hardware.

PREFLOW gives the operator a parameter with direct semantics instead. The
parameter controls the maximum modeled inflation over the FCFS service a request
could expect from the state it saw at arrival.

## PREFLOW in three steps

PREFLOW makes one scheduling decision at each chunk boundary.

1. Freeze the request's arrival-state FCFS baseline. For request $i$,

   $$
   B_i = \text{predicted FCFS completion latency from the state at arrival}.
   $$

   This baseline includes unfinished work ahead of the request and counts any
   outstanding chunk exactly once. Later arrivals cannot enlarge it.

2. Convert that baseline into an immutable completion entitlement:

   $$
   D_i = a_i + \rho B_i.
   $$

   Here $a_i$ is the logical arrival time. Setting $\rho=1.3$ allows at
   most 30% modeled completion inflation over that request's arrival-state FCFS
   baseline. The experiments use $\rho\in\{1.3,1.5,1.8,2.2\}$.

3. Rank requests by the work-aware objective and execute the highest-ranked
   chunk that preserves every fixed deadline.

The implementation ranks safe requests by

$$
\pi_i(t)=\frac{1}{p_i r_i(t)},
$$

where $p_i$ is the request's isolated prefill work and $r_i(t)$ is its
remaining work. This is the weighted-shortest-remaining-work index for total
stretch. Stretch is useful when request sizes differ by orders of magnitude: an
extra second is an 11× completion time for a request that needs 0.1 seconds in
isolation, but only 1.1× for one that needs 10 seconds.

The score $1/(p_i r_i)$ is the weighted-shortest-remaining-work priority
induced by the stretch objective. PREFLOW applies that priority only inside the
feasible action set. If the highest-ranked chunk is unsafe, it tries the next
candidate. An earliest-deadline action is the rescue choice when the queue has
no slack for a preferred overtake.

## Hard protection changes the benefit–harm tradeoff

The main comparison plots mean TTFT improvement against the largest observed
matched-request slowdown. Farther right means lower mean TTFT; lower means less
harm to the worst request. Lines connect measured parameter settings only. The
plot contains no fitted or extrapolated policy curves.

![Measured TTFT benefit versus worst-request harm](benchmark_output/final-synthetic-policy-suite/analysis/05_benefit_fairness_tradeoff.png)

The measured operating points are:

| Policy | Mean TTFT improvement over FCFS | Worst matched-request slowdown |
|---|---:|---:|
| SRPT | 84.6% | 7.52× |
| SJF | 81.7% | 6.68× |
| PrefillOnly $\lambda=200$ | 80.4% | 5.62× |
| PrefillOnly $\lambda=500$ | 60.9% | 4.38× |
| PrefillOnly $\lambda=2000$ | 24.4% | 3.63× |
| EDF 120% | 5.8% | 1.60× |
| PREFLOW 30% | 68.2% | 1.37× |
| PREFLOW 50% | 73.2% | 1.66× |
| PREFLOW 80% | 76.1% | 1.92× |
| PREFLOW 120% | 78.2% | 2.37× |

The central comparison is PREFLOW against PrefillOnly. PREFLOW 120% gives up
2.2 percentage points of mean improvement relative to PrefillOnly
$\lambda=200$, while reducing the worst observed slowdown from 5.62× to
2.37×. PREFLOW 30% is better on both measured axes than the most protective
tested PrefillOnly setting: 68.2% versus 24.4% mean improvement and 1.37×
versus 3.63× worst slowdown.

SRPT minimizes mean flow time in the classical known-size preemptive
single-server model and gives the highest measured mean-TTFT improvement among
the policies in this experiment. We therefore use it as the aggressive
efficiency reference, not as a formal bound for the real PD implementation.
PREFLOW 120% retains about 92% of SRPT's measured mean-TTFT improvement while
cutting worst observed slowdown by more than threefold.

EDF is the ablation for the other half of the design. It uses the same frozen
deadlines but always follows deadline order, removing PREFLOW's greedy choice
among safe actions. Its 5.8% mean improvement shows that deadlines provide
protection, while the work-aware choice among safe actions produces most of
the latency benefit.

These points describe the tradeoff measured on this workload and seed. They do
not establish a complete Pareto frontier.

## A hard modeled constraint produces a different tail

The slowdown tail answers the practical version of "why not tune aging?" A
softer priority can make large slowdowns rarer. It cannot make the next unsafe
overtake illegal.

There is one important distinction before reading the figure. PREFLOW enforces
deadlines in charged, modeled work units inside a run. The plot divides observed
wall-clock TTFT under PREFLOW by observed TTFT for the matched request in a
separate FCFS execution. The numerator therefore includes cost-model error,
asynchronous execution, admission effects, KV transfer, and run-to-run noise;
the denominator also differs from the frozen internal baseline $B_i$.

For that reason, the measured endpoints are 1.369×, 1.658×, 1.920×, and
2.365× for configured modeled bounds of 1.3×, 1.5×, 1.8×, and 2.2×. The
figure evaluates how the modeled constraint translates to observed wall-clock
latency. It is not a direct test of the theorem in its native units.

![FCFS-relative slowdown tails](benchmark_output/final-synthetic-policy-suite/analysis/05b_fcfs_slowdown_tail_behavior.png)

The horizontal axis is observed TTFT divided by matched FCFS TTFT. The vertical
axis is the fraction of all requests slower than that ratio, on a logarithmic
scale.

SJF, SRPT, and all three PrefillOnly settings produce tapering tails. Stronger
aging shortens the observed tail, but every additional overtake remains a legal
action. The endpoints from 3.63× to 7.52× are finite observations from this
trace, not policy limits.

PREFLOW has a different shape. Each curve keeps a broad region of useful
reordering and then ends near its configured FCFS-relative boundary. Once an
earlier deadline has no prefix slack left, another bypass is illegal. Changing
$\rho$ moves that boundary; it does not change the rule that enforces it.

## Queue behavior during bursts

Scheduling cannot create prefill compute. It can change which requests finish
first, and that changes a request-count queue because short requests leave the
system faster than long ones.

![Prefill queue trajectories](benchmark_output/final-synthetic-policy-suite/analysis/12_prefill_queue_trajectories.png)

FCFS carries 52.9 waiting requests on average and peaks at 128. EDF behaves
similarly, averaging 49.7, because its cumulative FCFS-relative deadlines
largely preserve arrival order. SRPT averages 6.5 waiting requests and peaks at
22 by draining short requests first, while PREFLOW 120% averages 9.7 with a
2.37× rather than 7.52× worst matched slowdown. Across the PREFLOW settings,
mean waiting occupancy ranges from 15.0 at 30% slack to 9.7 at 120%.

PrefillOnly's queue is sensitive to $\lambda$: mean waiting occupancy grows
from 9.2 requests at $\lambda=200$ to 39.6 at $\lambda=2000$. This is the
same tuning tradeoff seen in latency.

Every panel returns to zero near the end of the run. The workload contains
temporary bursts, but the system eventually processes all offered work. Lower
queue depth here reflects completion order, not extra capacity or admission
drops.

## How the guarantee works

PREFLOW treats queue slack as an overtaking budget. At a chunk boundary, sort
all unfinished released requests by fixed deadline. For the first $k$
requests in that earliest-deadline-first (EDF) order, define

$$
\sigma_k(t)=D_{(k)}-t-\sum_{j=1}^{k}r_{(j)}(t).
$$

The deadline minus the current clock is the available service; the sum is the
work the prefix still needs. A nonnegative $\sigma_k$ means the prefix remains
feasible.

For a candidate at EDF position $m$ with next-chunk cost $q$, every earlier
prefix loses $q$ units of slack. Prefixes containing the candidate lose the
same amount of time and remaining work, so their slack is unchanged. The chunk
is safe exactly when

$$
q\le\min_{k<m}\sigma_k(t).
$$

Prefix slack is the remaining overtaking budget. The earliest-deadline request
has no earlier prefix, so it provides a rescue action whenever a preferred
overtake is unsafe.

The arrival-state FCFS continuation supplies the initial feasible schedule.
Accepted chunks preserve prefix feasibility, and later arrivals cannot change
older deadlines. Under conservative cost and admission assumptions, request
$i$ therefore completes by $D_i=a_i+\rho B_i$. The full proofs are in the
[preprint source](preflow_preprint.tex). The corresponding implementation
remains the existing
[PREFLOWScheduler](../../../vllm_ascend/core/preflow_scheduler.py).

## Implementation details and limits

Token-count and triangular attention proxies can be useful for ranking requests.
They are insufficient for enforcing a meaningful completion-inflation contract:
the feasibility test spends predicted action cost as time, so the charged units
must track actual service time.

The reported PREFLOW results therefore use the startup-profiled device-time
model. An empty prefill worker profiles the real model path and fits a monotone,
history-dependent predictor in milliseconds using the features $1,h,h^2$.
Sparse measurements cover chunks smaller than the configured size. Summing
these predictions gives isolated work, remaining work, and next-action cost.

Calibration requests and their KV state are removed before serving begins. The
scheduling principle does not depend on this particular predictor; another
additive device-time model could replace it without changing the deadline or
prefix-slack rules.

KV admission adds another blocking point. PREFLOW reserves a conservative
allowance for the resident set to drain; once a waiting request becomes
admission-critical, younger work cannot enlarge its blocking set. The normal KV
allocator and waiting/running lifecycle remain unchanged.

The theorem is exact in the scheduler's charged model units. The current
profiler fits median point estimates rather than conservative upper bounds, so
it does not establish the same bound in wall-clock time. Charging an empirical
upper quantile or measured error envelope would tighten that translation
without redesigning the scheduler.

This evaluation also has a narrow scope: one synthetic trace and seed, dummy
weights, one hardware topology, and no prefix caching. Real traces, prefix
reuse, and other platforms remain to be evaluated. The guarantee applies to
prefill service under the stated model and admission assumptions.

## The prefill advantage remains visible end to end

Decode adds another stage after the scheduler-controlled prefill. The same
benefit/harm separation remains visible when we measure full end-to-end latency
for the 128-token outputs.

![Measured E2E benefit versus worst-request harm](benchmark_output/final-synthetic-policy-suite/analysis/05c_e2e_benefit_fairness_tradeoff.png)

PREFLOW spans 66.6% to 76.7% mean E2E improvement, with worst observed E2E
slowdown from 1.43× to 2.30×. PrefillOnly $\lambda=200$ reaches 78.8% mean
improvement with 5.38× worst slowdown. SRPT reaches 83.4% and 6.95×.

These are measured two-stage results. PREFLOW provides a prefill completion
guarantee; it does not provide a full E2E guarantee for downstream decode.

## Conclusion

Preemption fixes head-of-line blocking by moving some requests ahead of others.
The unresolved question is how much delay those overtakes may impose on the
requests left behind.

Aging modifies the priority and relies on tuning to shape the resulting tail.
PREFLOW constrains the legal schedule. The work-aware objective still chooses
the highest-priority next action, but only inside a safety envelope that
preserves every fixed FCFS-relative entitlement already in the system.
